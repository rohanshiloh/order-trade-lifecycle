1 · The lifecycle at a glance

At 9:30 on the morning of August 1, 2012, a piece of dead code woke up on one of Knight Capital's eight order routers. A technician had missed the server during a deployment, and an obsolete test module — one designed to generate child orders without limit — began doing exactly that against live markets. For forty-five minutes Knight's systems fired millions of orders into the New York Stock Exchange, accumulating unwanted positions in 154 stocks. By the time someone pulled the plug, the firm had lost roughly $460 million — about ten million dollars a minute — and one of the largest market makers in America needed a rescue by the weekend. The SEC's order in the Knight matter (2013) remains the best short case study in what happens when the machinery described in this document — order routing, child-order generation, kill switches, reconciliation of parent against child — fails all at once.

Nobody at Knight had fat-fingered a ticket. Every individual message was well-formed, and the router routed exactly as instructed. What failed was not routing but state: eight servers no longer agreed on which code was live, and no system in the chain could answer the only question that mattered — which orders should exist right now, and on whose authority. What failed, in other words, was the lifecycle: the chain of custody that is supposed to bind every order a machine sends to an intention a human actually had. Understanding that chain — what the systems are, who owns which state, and what travels on the wire between them — is the subject of what follows.

Every institutional trade is really two intertwined lifecycles. The order lifecycle is the front-office journey of an instruction: an intention to buy or sell that is created, checked, routed, sliced, worked, and finally extinguished by fills or cancellation. The trade lifecycle begins the instant a fill occurs: it is the journey of an economic event — an executed quantity at a price — through allocation, confirmation, clearing, and settlement, until cash and securities actually move. A useful mental test: the boundary is economic exposure. Up to the moment an execution creates it, changes are order-state transitions — amend, cancel, expire. Once it exists, changes are handled instead as trade corrections, cancels or busts, which is a different mechanism with different participants. FIX reflects the boundary through the execution-report workflow rather than stating it outright: trade and trade-correction events — F, G and H in most equity workflows — belong to the trade side, while most other ExecType(150) values describe order state. Brokers extend the vocabulary, so read the counterparty's rules of engagement rather than the base spec.

Key concept — the boundary is economic exposure

The usual shorthand is that the order lifecycle covers anything still cancellable. Useful intuition, not quite true — venues bust and correct trades after the fact. The exact line is economic exposure. Up to the moment an execution creates it, changes are order-state transitions: amend, cancel, expire, all of them routine and all of them reversing an intention that had not yet cost anything. Once exposure exists, the instrument of change is different — a trade correction, cancel or bust, involving the venue and sometimes the clearing house, and leaving a record that something was undone rather than never done. Everything in this paper sits on one side of that line or the other.

The two lifecycles run at radically different speeds. The order lifecycle plays out in microseconds to minutes across the OMS, EMS, and venues; the trade lifecycle plays out in hours to a full settlement cycle (T+1 for US equities) across middle office, CCPs, and depositories. The systems, message types, and data models split along this same seam.

2 · Market structure: the terrain the lifecycle crosses

On the evening of Friday, 24 October 1986, the London Stock Exchange still worked much as it had for two centuries: brokers carried client orders to jobbers who made prices face to face, commissions were fixed by agreement, and the two roles were kept rigidly apart. By Monday morning all three arrangements were gone. Fixed commissions were abolished, single capacity ended, and prices moved onto screens — the SEAQ system — where they could be seen without standing on the floor. Within weeks that floor was effectively deserted, its traders relocated to desks; within a few years most of the venerable British broking houses had been swallowed by American and continental banks. The reform arrived in a single stroke and is still called, without much exaggeration, the Big Bang. The name has stuck so thoroughly that it obscures how deliberate the change was: it was the settlement of a restrictive-practices case the government had brought against the Exchange, traded away in return for wholesale deregulation. Market structure is rarely an accident.

The lesson generalises, and it is worth stating plainly before the machinery of the following sections: the shape of the order lifecycle is not a fact of nature. It is a policy artefact. Where orders may go, what must be displayed, who may trade with whom, and how quickly the resulting trade must settle are all written down somewhere by a regulator — and each time that writing changes, the systems change with it.

America travelled the same road by a different route, and largely by scandal. In 1994 two academics noticed that Nasdaq market makers almost never quoted in odd eighths, a statistical impossibility absent some tacit understanding among dealers. The investigation that followed produced the 1997 Order Handling Rules, which forced dealers to display customer limit orders — or hand them to a venue that would. That second option was the loophole that became an industry: the electronic communication network, or ECN, a venue that was nothing but a matching engine and a fee schedule, crossing buyers against sellers directly with no dealer in the middle. Instinet, Island, Archipelago and their rivals gave the incumbent exchanges the first real competition they had ever faced. The category then dissolved by winning. Archipelago became NYSE Arca; Island and INET were absorbed into Nasdaq. What began as alternatives to exchanges became the exchanges, and their model — no floor, no specialists, price-time priority in software — is simply how equities trade now. The term survives in active use mainly in FX. Decimalisation in 2001 then cut the minimum tick to a penny, which narrowed spreads and simultaneously thinned displayed size, making large orders harder to work in the open and pushing block flow into the dark. Reg ATS had already legitimised those dark venues; Reg NMS — the Regulation National Market System — finally wired the resulting archipelago together by making it illegal to ignore a better price elsewhere. Christie and Schultz's 1994 paper on Nasdaq's missing odd-eighth quotes is one of the rare pieces of academic microstructure research to have directly rewritten a market's rules — and, indirectly, to have created an industry.

Key concept — fragmentation

A single instrument no longer trades in one place. A liquid U.S. equity has quotes resting simultaneously on a dozen-plus lit exchanges and prints crossing in thirty-odd dark venues, each with its own fee schedule, matching rules, and latency to your data centre. "The price" is therefore a construction — the national best bid and offer, assembled from feeds that arrive at different moments — and reconstructing it faithfully is harder than it sounds, since a venue quoting through the NBBO would otherwise set its own benchmark. Fragmentation is the reason the smart order router exists, the reason latency became an execution variable rather than an infrastructure detail, and the reason best execution must be measured rather than asserted.

Key concept — smart order routing (SOR)

The software that answers, for one order that must be executed now, the question which venue or venues, and in what proportion. It consults live depth across every connected book, historical fill probability, fee and rebate schedules, and its own venue-quality statistics, and may post, sweep, or split across several destinations at once. Reg NMS made it effectively compulsory in the U.S.: once trading through a better displayed price became illegal, every broker needed a machine that continuously knew all the displayed prices and could reach them. Section 4 takes the SOR apart properly; it is named here because the term is unavoidable from this point on.

Key concept — best execution

An obligation to seek the most favourable terms reasonably available for the client — not merely the best price, but price, cost, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, size, and nature, weighed together. It is a process obligation, which is why it lands squarely on the systems described here: a firm must be able to show with data how its router chose and how the outcome compared with the alternatives. Best-ex reporting is the regulatory reason the TCA of §4 and the queryable history of §8 are not optional extras.

This is the terrain across which everything that follows moves. The lifecycle described in the coming sections — parent order, compliance, slicing, routing, fills, allocation, clearing, settlement — is the trace left by a single intention crossing that fragmented, regulated, latency-differentiated landscape.

And it is worth following the causal chain one step further than most accounts do, because it explains why the companion paper exists at all. Fragmentation means there is more than one place to trade. More than one place means there is a choice, and choices can be made well or badly. Once execution quality varies, and varies by decisions a machine is making thousands of times a second, no one can assert that it was good — it has to be measured, continuously, against something. That measurement is transaction cost analysis, and it is not an accounting afterthought bolted onto trading. It is the direct, unavoidable consequence of a market structure that offers the router a decision to get wrong. Which is the hinge between the two papers. This one describes the machine that produces the choices; Anatomy of a Fill builds the apparatus that scores them. Neither is complete without the other: measurement with no model of the machine cannot say what to fix, and a machine with no measurement cannot know it is broken.

3 · The OMS: owner of the order

In December 2005, a trader at Mizuho Securities in Tokyo meant to sell one share of a newly listed company called J-Com at ¥610,000. He typed it the other way around: sell 610,000 shares at ¥1 — more than forty times the number of J-Com shares that existed. The order sailed through, the exchange's systems refused Mizuho's frantic cancellation attempts, and the bank lost about ¥40 billion unwinding a position in a company it had effectively sold several times over. The episode is remembered as the canonical fat-finger; it is better remembered as a failure of the checks that are supposed to stand between a human hand and the market. A warning dialog did appear on the Mizuho trader's screen. He clicked through it — the same dialog appeared routinely on legitimate orders. Alert fatigue is a lifecycle risk too: a control that fires on everything protects nothing.

That standing-between is the job of the Order Management System — the book of record for orders. Its concern is not how to trade well but whether the firm may trade at all, and how the resulting positions map onto accounts, funds, and books. Everything the OMS does is organised around the parent order — the full intended quantity, tied to a portfolio decision. On the sell side the analogous system is often called an order manager or order router within the broker's stack; the responsibilities (compliance, state of record, client allocation) are the same, with client orders in place of fund orders.

Key concept — the parent as unit of intent

The parent is an intention — buy 500k — not a market artefact; venues never see it. It exists so that compliance, allocation and attribution have a stable anchor while the EMS shreds that intention into hundreds or thousands of short-lived orders, each with its own identifier, its own state machine and a lifetime measured in milliseconds. Those are disposable, cancelled and re-placed constantly as the algo chases the market. The parent is not: its cumQty and avgPx only ever move forward, fill by fill. Lineage is what holds the two together, and the invariant parent.cum = Σ child.cum is the single most important reconciliation in the front office — it is what lets a blotter show one intention rather than a blizzard of placements.

The lifecycle inside the OMS runs: creation (from a rebalance, model signal, or manual ticket) → pre-trade compliance (restricted lists, concentration and exposure limits, mandate rules, regulatory checks) → staging and routing (direct to broker, to an algo wheel, or handed to the EMS) → state tracking as fills arrive → allocation of the completed quantity across accounts → enrichment and hand-off to post-trade. A compliance rejection kills the order before it ever leaves the firm; this is the cheapest possible place to stop a bad trade.

There is a second, subtler thing the OMS is guarding, and the best illustration of it nearly destroyed one of the great Wall Street houses. Paul Mozer ran the government-bond desk at Salomon Brothers, and he was irritated by a Treasury rule capping any single bidder at 35% of an auction. So he bid in the names of clients who had not authorised it — in one 1991 auction taking effective control of some 94% of the issue — and when the phantom bids won, he moved the securities into Salomon's own book behind fabricated paperwork. Senior management learned of it and sat on the knowledge for months. When it surfaced, the firm came within days of failing; Warren Buffett stepped in as interim chairman and told Congress, in a formulation that has outlived the scandal, that he could forgive a trader for losing money but not for losing the firm's reputation. Salomon paid roughly $290 million and lost its chief executive; Mozer served four months. The enduring legacy is in the plumbing rather than the punishment — on whose behalf became a field that systems must carry, validate, and be able to prove after the fact.

The point for this document is that Mozer's fraud was not a pricing failure or an execution failure. It was an attribution failure: orders that claimed an authority they did not have, in a system with no way to check the claim. Every "on behalf of" field on a modern order ticket, every capacity flag, every client-consent check in a pre-trade compliance engine descends from episodes like this one. An order is not merely a quantity and a price; it is an assertion about who is entitled to make it, and the OMS is where that assertion is recorded and tested.

Allocation deserves emphasis because it is where the order lifecycle physically becomes several trade lifecycles. A 500k-share parent worked for eight funds becomes, post-fill, eight allocated trades with distinct accounts, custodians, and settlement instructions — each of which confirms, clears, and settles independently from that point on. Block trading desks invert the sequence: execute the block first at an average price, allocate afterwards. FIX supports both pre-trade allocation (allocations carried on the order) and post-trade allocation (AllocationInstruction, 35=J) — post-trade is overwhelmingly the norm.

4 · The EMS: owner of the execution

On the afternoon of May 6, 2010, a mutual fund complex in Kansas needed to hedge, and its execution algorithm was given one instruction: sell 75,000 E-mini S&P futures — about $4.1 billion — at 9% of market volume, with no limit on price and no regard for time. As the selling pushed prices down, volatility rose; as volatility rose, volume exploded; and as volume exploded, an algorithm calibrated only to volume sold faster into a market that was fleeing. In twenty minutes the Dow fell nearly a thousand points. Accenture printed at one cent; Sotheby's at $99,999.99. Then, almost as quickly, it all came back. The joint SEC–CFTC report on the Flash Crash traced the cascade in detail. The stub-quote prints — a blue-chip at a penny — were market-maker placeholder quotes never meant to trade, exposed when every real bid withdrew at once. Roughly 20,000 trades were later cancelled.

The Flash Crash is what an execution algorithm looks like with its feedback loop miswired: a benchmark (participation) pursued faithfully while the thing that mattered (price) went unwatched. It is worth keeping in mind through this section, because everything an EMS does — benchmarks, urgency, slicing, routing — is ultimately about wiring that loop correctly.

Where the OMS answers may we, and for whom, the Execution Management System answers how. It receives the parent (or a slice of it), selects an execution strategy against a benchmark — VWAP, TWAP, POV, implementation shortfall, close — and generates child orders: small, short-lived, venue-facing orders sized and timed by the algo. The EMS is where real-time market data, smart order routing, and execution analytics converge. The OMS/EMS boundary is increasingly commercial rather than architectural — vendors sell merged OEMS platforms. But even inside an OEMS the data model keeps the split: parent-order state with compliance and allocation context on one side, child-order and execution-report state on the other.

The smart order router sits below the algo: given a child order to place now, it chooses venues using real-time depth, historical fill probability, fees and rebates, and dark-pool toxicity metrics, and may spray, sweep, or post across several venues at once. Each placement, amend, and cancel is itself a full miniature order lifecycle, often lasting milliseconds.

Key concept — execution is a control loop

An algo is a feedback controller: target schedule in, child orders out, fills and market data back in, placement adjusted. Intraday TCA (slippage vs arrival, participation drift, venue fill quality) is the observable the trader uses to intervene. This is precisely a complex-event-processing problem — joining a stream of execution reports against streaming quotes at event time — which is why EMS internals and kdb+ CEP engines look so much alike.

Key concept — one word, three vocabularies

The same object travels under different names depending on who is speaking. A buy-side EMS calls it a child order; a broker's algo container calls it an algo order; a kdb+ schema calls it an algoChild row whose poid points at the parent. Two of these deserve emphasis because they are routinely merged. The algo order and the placement look alike — both are smaller than the parent, both are short-lived — but they answer different questions and belong to different systems. The algo order says work two thousand shares over the next thirty seconds. The placement says and do it by resting five hundred on Arca. One algo order typically produces several placements as the router posts, cancels and re-posts; measuring across that boundary is how you find out whether a bad execution was a scheduling decision or a routing one.

The execution stack, and what an order really becomes

Figure 2 tells a convenient lie. It shows a parent order becoming child orders, which is how the lifecycle is usually described and how the FIX vocabulary of ClOrdID chains encourages you to think. Inside a real execution platform there are not two levels but four, and the middle two belong to different pieces of software with different responsibilities. Conflating them is the single most common source of arguments about whose fault a bad execution was.

Two components carry those middle levels. The trading engine is the process that runs the strategy: it holds the working parent, executes the schedule logic, consumes market data and execution reports, and emits algoChildren. The order distributor sits above it and is the piece most often misunderstood, because its name suggests it distributes orders to the market. It does not. It distributes them to your own processes — deciding which trading engine instance, out of a farm of them, will handle a given order. Vocabulary here is stubbornly local. What one firm calls a trading engine another calls an algo container, algo engine, strategy engine, or execution engine; an order distributor may appear as an order router (confusingly), order gateway, dispatcher, or simply the strategy manager. The functions are near-universal even where the names are not, so read the responsibilities rather than the labels.

Why bother with a distributor at all? Because a single engine process cannot carry the whole desk, and the moment you have several, you must decide which one owns which order — and get it right, because the alternative is disastrous. If two engines could both work orders in the same symbol, they would need to coordinate over shared state on every tick, which means locks, which means latency and deadlocks in the hot path. The industry's answer is to make coordination unnecessary by construction: shard the universe so each engine owns a disjoint slice of it, and pin every order in a slice to that engine — its affinity.

Key concept — sharding and affinity

Sharding splits a workload into disjoint slices — typically by symbol — so that each process owns its slice exclusively. Affinity is the rule that a given order always goes to the process owning its slice, and stays there for life. Together they buy something precious in a latency-sensitive system: no two processes ever need to agree about anything, so there are no locks, no consensus round-trips, and no chance of two engines double-working the same book. The cost is that a hot symbol cannot be spread across engines — you scale by adding shards, not by splitting one.

The assumption underneath is that an order's state is confined to one symbol — true of single-stock parents, false of pairs, baskets and cash-neutral portfolio algos, which need a view across symbols that no single shard holds. Those either shard by portfolio or basket id instead, or keep a thin risk layer above the symbol engines that owns the cross-symbol exposure and leaves the per-symbol work where it is.

Key concept — the order distributor routes inwards

The most common confusion in this layer is directional. An order distributor routes orders to your own engine processes; a smart order router routes orders to external venues. One is a load-balancing and ownership problem inside your data centre, solved with hashes and shard maps; the other is an execution problem out in the market, solved with depth, fees, and latency measurements. They sit adjacently in the stack and share the word "route", which is why they are so often conflated in conversation and so rarely conflated in code.

The routing problem

Around 2007, a Canadian trader named Brad Katsuyama noticed something that should not have been happening. He ran the trading desk at Royal Bank of Canada's New York office — RBC being, by the standards of lower Manhattan, an institution of almost provincial politeness, a bank whose internal culture prized not screwing the client over screwing the competition, which turns out to matter to how this story ends. He would see 100,000 shares offered across several exchanges, press the button to buy all of it, and get a fraction of it at a worse price while the remainder evaporated in front of him. The liquidity was real. It simply stopped being available in the instant between his deciding to take it and his order arriving to do so.

No one on a trading floor could explain this, because it was not a trading phenomenon; it was a plumbing one. So Katsuyama went outside his own profession and hired a man no equities desk would otherwise have employed: Ronan Ryan, an Irishman who had spent his career in telecommunications — pulling fibre, leasing data-centre cages, thinking in milliseconds and route miles rather than in spreads and volumes. A Canadian who knew what was happening and an Irish telecoms engineer who knew why turned out to be exactly the pair the problem required. The diagnosis was embarrassingly physical: the exchanges sit in different buildings scattered across northern New Jersey, Katsuyama's order legs departed simultaneously but travelled unequal distances of glass, and so they arrived unequally. The first arrival was a starting pistol. Whoever heard it could reach the remaining venues before his own slower legs did. Michael Lewis's Flash Boys (2014) made this episode famous and is fiercely contested — on whether the practice was predatory, on how much it cost investors, and on whether IEX's remedy was the right one. The underlying engineering fact is not contested by anyone: legs of a sweep arrive when the speed of light and the fibre route say they will, not when you sent them.

Their fix, a router christened THOR, was counter-intuitive to the point of heresy on a desk that measured itself in speed: deliberately send the legs at different times, holding back the ones with the shortest path, so that all of them land at once. Slowing parts of the order down made the whole order faster in the only sense that mattered. The idea later hardened into IEX's thirty-eight coiled miles of fibre. But the lesson outlives that particular quarrel: a smart order router is not a lookup of who has the best price. It is a scheduling problem inside a system where news of your own intentions propagates at a finite and knowable speed.

That is one decision among several. Every child order the router handles poses two questions before the venue question is even reached: whether to post (rest passively, wait to be traded with) or take (cross the spread now), and how much to show. Only then does venue selection follow, constrained in the U.S. by Reg NMS, which forbids executing at a price inferior to another venue's protected quote. Rule 611, the Order Protection Rule — adopted in 2005, phased in through 2007 — is the reason the modern SOR exists as a distinct piece of software. Once trading through a better displayed price became illegal, every broker needed a machine that continuously knew all the displayed prices and could reach them. Fragmentation and the router were born together, by regulation.

Key concept — post or take

Every other routing choice is a refinement of this one. Posting rests an order and waits: you earn the spread, may collect a rebate, and pay nothing in impact — but you may not trade at all, and when you do it is often because someone better informed chose to trade with you. Taking crosses the spread: certain, immediate, and you pay for it in spread, fees, and a visible print. Urgency is simply where the algo sits on this axis, re-decided for every child order as the book changes.

Key concept — internalisation

Before a router considers any venue, many brokers first check their own book: can this order be crossed against house inventory, or against another client's opposite order sitting in the same system? An internalised fill never touches the street. It saves exchange fees and leaks nothing to the market, and for a client it can be the best available outcome. The price can still move afterwards — internalisation is not immunity from impact — but whatever moves it was not a venue picking you off. It also means the broker is taking the other side of its own client's trade, which is why internalisation carries disclosure obligations, why the SI regime exists in Europe, and why internalised fills must be scored separately in any honest TCA — a house fill at the touch and a street fill at the touch are not the same trade, and averaging them together hides exactly the thing you want to measure. Make it a column, not a footnote: a liquidity flag on every fill recording whether you removed lit liquidity, added it, matched at a dark midpoint, or crossed internally — and see why the companion scores it apart.

Key concept — the maker-taker conflict

Most lit venues pay a rebate for posting and charge a fee for taking. Since the broker keeps the difference but the client bears the execution outcome, a router can be tuned to maximise rebate capture rather than fill quality — resting where the economics are best rather than where the fill is. Regulators have repeatedly found exactly this, along with dark pools that misdescribed who was trading in them; several venue operators paid nine-figure settlements in 2016 over such disclosures. It is the clearest case in the lifecycle where the routing logic and the client's interest can quietly diverge, and why venue-level execution statistics are worth computing yourself.

Which is why the last box in the loop matters most: a serious desk scores every venue continuously — fill rates, effective spread paid, adverse selection measured by post-fill reversion, queue position achieved — and feeds those scores back into the routing table. The router is only as good as the statistics it is judged by, and those statistics are exactly the ones the next subsection defines.

Key concept — slippage

Execution price versus a reference price, in basis points, signed so that positive means you paid. The reference defines the metric: arrival price (mid at parent-order creation) gives implementation shortfall — the cost of the whole decision-to-done gap; interval VWAP measures the algo against the market's own average; close suits funds benchmarked to NAV. Arrival slippage is the honest one — it captures delay, impact, and spread together, and it is what the aj against the quote stream in §8 computes fill by fill.

Key concept — drift

The adverse trend component of price movement over the execution horizon — the market walking away from you while you work. For a buy order in a rising tape, every minute of patience costs; arrival slippage grows even if each fill is executed beautifully. Drift is why urgency exists as an algo parameter: trading faster buys less drift exposure at the cost of more impact. Schedule-tracking algos also speak of participation drift — deviation from the target participation curve — an execution-quality alarm rather than a price concept.

Key concept — reversion

What price does after your last fill. If you bought, pushed the price up, and it falls back within minutes, the move was temporary impact — you paid for liquidity, and the reversion measures the overpayment. If price stays up, the impact was permanent: the market read information in your flow. Post-trade reversion at horizons of one to thirty minutes is the standard diagnostic separating the two, and chronic reversion against your fills is the classic signature of trading too aggressively — or of being detected.

Key concept — cost never travels alone

A slippage number without its fill rate beside it is not a result, it is half of one: +4 bps on 20% filled is a worse outcome than −1 bps on 98%, and the first looks better in every report ever written. The same holds for a mean without its median — one busted print at −400 bps moves a venue's average and leaves its median untouched, so when the two disagree you have a tail, not a trend. Publish the qualifier in the same row as the number, or someone will eventually quote the number alone.

Key concept — opportunity cost

Every benchmark that scores only the shares you traded quietly rewards an algo for not trading: do nothing, fill nothing, and your slippage is immaculate. The missing term is what the unfilled remainder would have cost. Take the drift over the working window, weight it by the fraction you failed to execute, and report it alongside the realised cost — an order that captured a beautiful price on 30% of a position while the market ran away from the other 70% has not done well, and only this number says so.

Markouts: what the fill looks like afterwards

Markouts at 1s / 10s / 60s computes these curves off a live stream — the same aj as arrival slippage, run against a shifted timestamp. The curve figure there is drawn from real stack output rather than, as here, from illustration.

Benchmarks score the decision to trade. Markouts score the counterparty you traded with. The construction is simple: take the mid-quote at a series of horizons after each fill — conventionally around one second, ten seconds, and sixty — and compare it to the fill price, signed by side so that positive means the market moved in your favour afterwards. Aggregate those curves by venue and a picture emerges that no aggregate slippage number will ever show you. Sign conventions differ between desks and vendors — some quote markouts from the perspective of the liquidity provider, flipping every number. Fix the convention once, write it down, and put it in the column header; more TCA arguments start here than anywhere else. The companion settles it in a single helper and documents the one exception it deliberately keeps — effective and realised spread stay on the microstructure convention rather than silently inverting the published literature.

A venue whose markouts hover near zero is matching you against uninformed flow — a clean fill. A venue whose markouts fall steadily negative is selecting against you: the counterparties there know something, and the price keeps moving after they trade with you. That is adverse selection, and at scale it dwarfs the fee differences that routing tables are so often tuned around. The shape matters as much as the level: damage that appears by one second and then stops is latency arbitrage — someone faster reacted to a change you hadn't seen. Damage that keeps accruing out to a minute is genuine information — you traded with someone who knew where the price was going.

Key concept — markouts and venue toxicity

A markout is the mid-quote at t + Δ minus the fill price, signed by side. Computed per venue and averaged over thousands of fills, the Δ = 1s / 10s / 60s triple is the standard test for adverse selection — in practice the most actionable number in all of TCA, because unlike slippage it is attributable to a specific routing decision you can change tomorrow. Toxicity is not a property of lit versus dark; it is a property of who else is in the pool, which is why it must be measured continuously rather than assumed from the venue type.

Key concept — book state at the moment of placement

Benchmarks and markouts both treat the fill as a point. The features that explain why a fill went the way it did live in the book at the instant the child order was placed: depth on each side, the resting size at the touch, the queue ahead of you, spread in ticks, and above all order-flow imbalance — the signed net change in bid versus ask depth over the preceding window, which is among the strongest short-horizon predictors of the next price move. Capture these as columns on the placement record at placement time; they cannot be reconstructed faithfully afterwards, and without them slippage analysis can describe what happened but never account for it.

Where every number attaches

It is worth pausing here, because this subsection and the last have been describing measurements without ever saying what they are measurements of. Every metric in transaction cost analysis belongs to exactly one level of the hierarchy in Figure 3, and most arguments about execution quality are really arguments between people reading numbers from different levels. Arrival slippage is a property of the parent — it indicts the decision and the delay. Schedule adherence belongs to the algoChild. Queue position and spread paid belong to the placement. Markouts belong to the venue. Settlement efficiency belongs to the trade, long after anyone in the front office has stopped looking.

The practical use of this is narrower than it looks and more valuable. When a number is bad, the first question is not why but whose row is this — and the second is whether the adjacent rows agree. Arrival slippage poor while participation tracks the curve and markouts sit near zero is a decision-timing problem, not an execution one. Markouts negative on one venue while queue position and spread are fine everywhere else is a routing problem with a single-line fix. The hierarchy turns a vague complaint into a located one.

Two systems, two temperaments

Step back from the analytics and the deeper contrast between the two systems comes into focus. The OMS and the EMS are not merely two products in a stack; they embody two different temperaments the front office needs simultaneously. One is a registrar — meticulous, conservative, allergic to ambiguity, whose worst nightmare is a position booked to the wrong fund. The other is a race-car driver — opportunistic, latency-obsessed, cheerfully discarding thousands of child orders a day, whose worst nightmare is being seen. Firms that let one temperament colonise the other end up with a compliance engine too slow to trade or an execution engine nobody can audit. The table below is really a character study. This is also the practical argument for keeping the parent/child split explicit in the data model even inside a merged OEMS: the registrar's tables need referential integrity and slow, audited change; the driver's tables need append-only speed. 5 · FIX: the connective tissue

In 1992, orders between Fidelity Investments and Salomon Brothers travelled the way they had for a century: a human voice on a telephone, followed by a paper ticket, followed — hours later, if ever — by confirmation that what was heard matched what was said. Two technologists, one at each firm, decided the interesting problem was not the trading but the telling, and sketched a message format so that Fidelity's machines could tell Salomon's machines directly. They called it the Financial Information eXchange. Within a decade the private experiment between two firms had become the lingua franca of global markets; today essentially every arrow in Figure 1's top lane is a FIX message, and a trader in Singapore can route to a broker in London she has never spoken to, because both sides agreed decades ago on what tag 55 means. The protocol is deliberately boring — flat tag=value pairs, integer tags, ASCII — and its boringness is the point. FIX outlived dozens of more elegant rivals because any firm could implement it in a weekend and debug it by reading the wire.

FIX is not a system in the chain so much as the protocol fabric the chain is woven from: OMS→EMS, EMS→broker, broker→venue, and much of post-trade all speak it. A FIX engine supplies the session layer — sequencing, heartbeats, gap-fill and resend, logon/logout — so that the application layer can treat the wire as a reliable, ordered stream of business messages. Session-layer subtleties leak into the lifecycle: a resend after a gap replays execution reports, so every consumer must be idempotent on ExecID(17); PossDupFlag(43) marks replays. Sequence-reset handling is where naive order-state engines corrupt themselves.

Key concept — what a FIX message actually is

A FIX message is a flat sequence of tag=value pairs separated by the SOH character (0x01): a standard header (8=FIX.4.4, 35=MsgType, sender/target comp IDs 49/56, sequence number 34), a body of business fields, and a trailing checksum (10). Tags are integers with globally agreed meanings — 55 is always Symbol, 44 always Price. Messages divide into session messages (logon, heartbeat, resend — the plumbing) and application messages (orders, executions, allocations — the business). Everything in the tables below is an application message riding on that session plumbing.

One message type — ExecutionReport (35=8) — spans both lifecycles, which is why FIX separates what just happened (ExecType, 150) from where the order now stands (OrdStatus, 39). An ack, a cancel confirmation, and a fill are all ExecutionReports; only ExecType=F creates a trade.

The order state machine

Every OMS, EMS, and exchange gateway maintains the same canonical state machine, driven by ExecType transitions and summarised in OrdStatus. Getting it right under partial fills, in-flight amends, and unsolicited cancels is most of the difficulty of order-state engineering. The invariant to enforce on every transition: OrderQty(38) = CumQty(14) + LeavesQty(151). When it breaks, you have missed, duplicated, or misordered an execution report.

Key concept — never key on ClOrdID

ClOrdID(11) is not an order identity, it is a message identity: every cancel and every amend mints a fresh one and points OrigClOrdID(41) at its predecessor. Key your internal order table on it and the first cancel-replace orphans every fill that came before — cumulative quantity resets, the blotter disagrees with the broker, and the reconciliation lands at midnight. Key instead on something immutable for the order's whole life: your own oid, or the venue's OrderID(37), with the ClOrdID chain stored alongside as lineage. This is why the schema in §8 keys ord on oid and nothing else.

Key concept — ExecType vs OrdStatus

ExecType(150) is the event: what this ExecutionReport announces. OrdStatus(39) is the state: where the order stands after applying it. Events are immutable and append-only; state is a fold over events. Persist the events, derive the state — never the reverse. (This is exactly the event-sourcing discipline, and FIX had it in the 1990s.)

6 · Post-trade: from fill to finality

In 1968 the New York Stock Exchange was drowning in its own success. Volume had tripled in a few years, every share still moved as an engraved paper certificate, and the back offices of Wall Street simply could not keep up: fails to deliver ballooned past $4 billion, certificates went missing by the sackful, and the Exchange took the extraordinary step of closing every Wednesday — shutting the largest market in the world one day a week — so clerks could dig out of the paper. More than a hundred member firms failed or were absorbed anyway. Out of the wreckage came the modern plumbing: certificates immobilised at a central depository (the DTC), obligations netted at a central clearing corporation (the NSCC), and the trade lifecycle as this section describes it. The Paperwork Crisis is why U.S. shares today exist mainly as electronic entries in the name of Cede & Co., DTC's nominee — a fact that surfaces to public astonishment every few decades, most recently during the meme-stock episode of 2021.

Once ExecType=F fires, the economics are agreed but nothing has actually moved. The trade lifecycle now runs a pipeline whose whole purpose is to turn a bilateral promise into settled cash and securities while compressing counterparty risk along the way: enrichment → allocation → confirmation/affirmation → clearing → settlement → reconciliation. US equities moved to T+1 in May 2024, which effectively made trade-date affirmation mandatory: the DTCC cutoff for affirmed trades to flow into night-cycle settlement is 9:00pm ET on T. Same-day allocation, once a courtesy, is now structural.

Allocation: one trade becomes many

The first thing that happens to a fill is that it stops being one thing. A block worked for eight funds is, the moment it is done, eight trades — different accounts, different custodians, different standing settlement instructions, each from here on settling independently and each capable of failing on its own. Allocation is the step that performs that division, and it is less arithmetic than it looks: the quantities rarely divide cleanly, average pricing has to be applied consistently so no fund is advantaged by the order in which fills arrived, and the residual share has to go somewhere defensible. Hence the preference for average-price allocation: every account receives the same avgPx regardless of which individual prints landed when. The alternative — handing specific fills to specific accounts — is legal, occasionally required, and a standing invitation to allegations of cherry-picking.

The deadline is what makes it hard. Under T+1 the allocation must be sent, the broker must confirm, and the investment manager must affirm, all before 9:00pm ET on trade date, because affirmed trades are what the depository's overnight cycle consumes. Miss it and the trade settles late or by exception, at cost. An allocation process that was a next-morning task for decades is now an evening one, and the compression is why central matching exists at all.

Confirmation, affirmation, and what "locked in" means

Confirmation is the broker stating the economics it believes it executed; affirmation is the investment manager agreeing. Until both have happened the trade is alleged — one side asserts it, the other has not yet accepted — and an alleged trade cannot settle. Central matching platforms collapse the two steps into one by matching allocation against confirmation automatically, so that the normal case produces an affirmed trade with no human in the path and the exceptions are what reach a person. The vocabulary is worth keeping straight because the industry uses it loosely. Confirmed is one-sided. Affirmed is bilateral agreement on the economics. Locked in means the trade has been accepted for clearing and can no longer be amended by either party — only corrected or busted through the venue's own process.

Once a trade is locked in, something subtle has changed: it is no longer a claim one party has against another, it is an obligation the clearing system has taken responsibility for sequencing. That hand-off is the real boundary of the middle office.

Key concept — why post-trade is underestimated

Front-office failure is loud: a bad fill shows up in a number someone is already watching. Post-trade failure is quiet and accrues. An allocation sent late costs nothing visible on the day; a fail costs a small financing charge; a reconciliation break sits in a queue. None of it reaches a P&L line with a name on it, which is exactly why the discipline erodes until something forces attention — a buy-in, a regulatory finding, or a cycle compression like T+1 that removes the slack the sloppiness was living in.

Key concept — three records of the same trade

Post-trade runs on the assumption that independently maintained records agree. Your books say one thing, the broker's confirm says another, the custodian's statement a third, and the depository holds the only version that is legally dispositive. Reconciliation is the continuous proof that all four describe the same world. The interesting failures are not the ones where a number is wrong but where two records are each internally consistent and mutually incompatible — which is why the timestamp and the identifier matter as much as the quantity.

Clearing: novation, netting, and margin

Clearing performs two compressions that are routinely conflated and are in fact unrelated. Novation is legal: the CCP interposes itself so that one trade between two members becomes two trades against the CCP, which is thereafter buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. Nobody's economics move; everybody's counterparty changes, from a name they did not choose to a single margined entity. Netting is arithmetic: within one security, one member and one settlement date, purchases and sales offset, and only the residual is delivered. The first compresses risk, the second compresses movement.

Margin is what makes novation survivable. Having made itself the counterparty to everyone, the CCP must be able to absorb a member's failure without passing the loss on, so it collects initial margin against potential future exposure and variation margin against today's moves, backed by a default fund and a waterfall specifying whose money is consumed in what order. Netting then does the arithmetic: well over 90% of gross obligations disappear, and a member who bought a million and sold nine hundred thousand of the same CUSIP across hundreds of trades settles one delivery of a hundred thousand.

The arrangement is extraordinarily effective, and it is not free — the same machinery produces both of its famous days. When Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, its cleared interest-rate book at LCH, some $9 trillion notional across tens of thousands of trades, was hedged and auctioned within weeks using a fraction of the margin Lehman had posted; the uncleared bilateral exposures took years of litigation to untangle. And in January 2021, when a crowd of retail traders ran GameStop vertical, it was the NSCC's margin call — billions of dollars, demanded of brokers before the open, against two further days of settlement exposure under the then-standard T+2 — that forced Robinhood and others to restrict buying. Clearing absorbed a systemic default in the first case and transmitted a liquidity shock in the second, by the same mechanism working exactly as designed. Which is the quiet argument for shortening the settlement cycle, and the direct line from January 2021 to May 2024. Margin scales with the exposure horizon; halve the horizon and you roughly halve what the CCP must demand when volatility spikes. T+1 was not primarily a convenience measure.

Asset classes diverge sharply here. FX settles PvP through CLS with no CCP for spot; much of fixed income clears through FICC with repo netting; cleared OTC derivatives (LCH, CME) have no "settlement" at all in the securities sense — the trade lifecycle becomes a decades-long stream of variation-margin cash flows. The front-office lifecycle is nearly asset-class-agnostic; post-trade is anything but.

Settlement, failure, and finality

Settlement is the only event in this entire document that is irreversible. Securities move against payment at the depository, and once that delivery-versus-payment exchange completes it cannot be unwound by either party — which is precisely the property the whole apparatus exists to reach. Everything upstream is provisional: amendable, cancellable, correctable, bustable. Finality is where provisionality ends.

Trades that do not settle on time fail, which is a technical state rather than a disaster — the seller could not deliver, usually because something they were owed has not arrived either. Fails are tracked, charged for, and eventually cured by buy-in, where the buyer goes to the market and the failing seller pays the difference. A chain of fails propagating through several members is the mechanism by which a settlement problem becomes a systemic one, and it is the reason the depository cares about the sequence of deliveries, not only their sum. Hence CNS being continuous: failed positions roll forward into the next day's netting rather than being handled as exceptions, so a fail is absorbed by the system's normal operation instead of requiring intervention.

Beneath all of it runs reconciliation — books against broker, broker against custodian, custodian against depository — continuously and in every direction, because the entire structure rests on independent records of the same facts agreeing. When they disagree, someone's position is wrong, and the question is only how long it takes to find out.

Key concept — netting is compression, settlement is finality

Everything between execution and settlement exists to manage the risk of the gap between promise and delivery. Netting shrinks the quantum that must move; margin collateralises what remains; affirmation deadlines shrink the window. Settlement finality — irrevocable DvP at the depository — is the only event that actually extinguishes the trade.

7 · Chronicle: the lifecycle at wire speed

For years the conventional wisdom held that serious trading systems could not be written in Java: sooner or later the garbage collector would pause the world — a few milliseconds, an eternity — precisely when the market was moving. A London-based engineer named Peter Lawrey took the opposite bet: keep the objects off the heap, map the memory straight to disk, never give the collector anything to collect, and Java could run with the C++ crowd. The result, open-sourced under the OpenHFT banner and commercialised as Chronicle Software, has become one of the quiet workhorses of the order lifecycle: when a child order leaves a bank's EMS in a microsecond and leaves an audit trail at the same time, there is a fair chance a Chronicle Queue is underneath it. Chronicle Queue passes messages between JVMs in under a microsecond, and between machines in under 10 µs with enterprise replication; Chronicle FIX parses or generates a FIX message in about 1 µs at the 99.9th percentile. For calibration: light travels roughly 300 metres in a microsecond.

The core idea is disarmingly simple. A Chronicle Queue is an append-only log in a memory-mapped file: a writer appends a message with no locks, no system call, no copy, and every reader — in the same process, another JVM, or a process that starts tomorrow — tails the same file at its own pace. Persistence is not a feature bolted onto the messaging; it is the messaging. Which means the three problems that dominate this document — recovery, audit, and determinism — collapse into one: the queue is simultaneously the transport, the database, and the compliance record.

Key concept — the queue is the system of record

In a Chronicle-style architecture no service holds authoritative state in memory. Each microservice is a pure function from input queues to output queues; its in-memory state is just a cache of everything it has read so far. Kill it, restart it, replay its inputs, and it must arrive at the same place — a property you can (and should) test in CI by diffing output queues. This is the same event-sourcing discipline FIX imposes with ExecutionReports and kdb+ imposes with the tickerplant log, enforced here at the level of every internal hop.

What makes that work is worth drawing, because the architecture is almost aggressively unremarkable — and the absences are the design. There is no broker process, so there is no network hop and no broker state machine in the path. There is no object graph on the Java heap, so the garbage collector never sees the data. There is no backpressure from reader to writer, because readers hold nothing but their own position in a file; a consumer that stalls falls behind and catches up, and the producer never learns it happened. Capacity is bounded by disk rather than by memory, which turns "replay a hundred terabytes" from an architectural problem into a storage one. Chronicle's own framing is a "record everything" queue behaving as a broker-less, durable, persisted JVM topic. The Enterprise line adds TCP replication for HA/DR, encryption, an Async Mode buffer that flattens tail latency under sustained load, and binary-compatible Java, C++, Rust and Python libraries reading the same files — a Python research process and a C++ execution process on one queue, with no translation layer between them.

An OMS/EMS built this way decomposes into a chain of small, single-threaded services connected by queues: a FIX gateway normalises client flow onto an input queue; a risk-and-compliance service reads it and emits accepted orders; an algo container slices; venue gateways speak FIX or native protocols outward; and every one of those hops is a durable log that can be tailed live by a blotter, a surveillance process, or — as the next section shows — a kdb+ feed handler, without the hot path ever knowing they exist. The single-writer principle does the heavy lifting: one thread owns each queue's head, so there is no contention to manage and no lock to take. Fan-in is handled by giving each producer its own queue and letting the consumer read several — queues are cheap.

The commercial suite fills in the rest of the lifecycle map: Chronicle FIX for the gateways at either end, Services as the microservice framework itself, Queue-Backed Map for replicated keyed state such as positions and open orders, a Market Data Distributor for the quote stream, and a Matching Engine framework for anyone who needs to be the venue. Crossing into kdb+

In February 2021 Chronicle announced it had integrated Chronicle Queue with kdb+, the work done in partnership with AquaQ Analytics — now Data Intellect — who open-sourced the result. The problem it solves is the one this section has been circling: Chronicle is superb at moving a message between Java processes in under a microsecond, and kdb+ is superb at storing and querying billions of them, and the two speak entirely different languages. Something has to stand between a memory-mapped queue of self-describing Java objects and a columnar database expecting bulk inserts. Peter Lawrey for Chronicle and Jonny Press, then CTO of AquaQ, announced it jointly on 5 February 2021. The adapter that came out of it is on GitHub as DataIntellectTech/kdb-chronicle-queue — a Producer, an Adapter and a dummy tickerplant, about which everything below is drawn from the source rather than the press release.

Chronicle's own vocabulary is three nouns. An excerpt is one message — a container of bytes in the mapped file. An appender writes them; a tailer reads them, sequentially, like an iterator that never reaches the end. That is the entire API surface you need to move data, and its smallness is the point: there is no broker, no topic metadata, no coordination protocol, just a file two processes have both mapped. Beware of version drift in the tutorials. Chronicle 3.x used ChronicleQueueBuilder.indexed(dir) with startExcerpt/finish and typed writeUTF calls; the 5.x line the adapter builds against uses SingleChronicleQueueBuilder, acquireAppender() and writeDocument(w -> …) with self-describing messages. The concepts survived the rewrite intact; the method names did not.

Key concept — the envelope, and two clocks

Chronicle's rhythm is one message, now. kdb+'s rhythm is many rows, together — a per-row IPC round trip would squander both systems' advantages at once. The envelope reconciles them: buffer rows on the Java side, send as a single Object[] when it fills or when the queue falls idle. The idle flush matters as much as the size threshold, since otherwise a quiet market leaves the last few quotes stranded in memory. This is the same batching logic a tickerplant applies on its own publish loop, implemented one hop earlier.

Key concept — read offset vs commit offset

The adapter keeps two named tailers: quoteTailer, tracking what has been read, and quoteSentTailer, tracking what kdb+ has actually acknowledged. Version 1.1.0 exists because the first release kept only the former — so a restart with a part-full envelope silently dropped those messages, which had been read but never stored. Splitting the two turns the envelope from a hole in the audit trail into a replayable window: on restart, rewind to the commit offset and re-send. Every durable pipeline eventually rediscovers this distinction; Kafka calls it the committed offset, and the consequence is the same — delivery becomes at-least-once, so the consumer must be idempotent, which is exactly what the execid dedupe in §8 provides.

One configuration detail repays attention, because it is the same idea as Figure 4 wearing different clothes. The adapter can filter on any string field — adapter.messageFilterField=sym with an inclusion or exclusion list — and the stated purpose is to let several adapter instances run independently. That is sharding: partition the symbol universe, give each adapter its own slice, and no two instances contend for the same work. The order distributor does it to trading engines; the adapter does it to database writers; the reasoning is identical both times.

One gap is worth naming before leaving it. The stack you just ran carries quote and trade — market data — because that is what Data Intellect's Producer generates. The lifecycle this paper is about travels on a third stream: execution reports. In production that is a second adapter instance with adapter.messageType=EXEC, or a FIX gateway appending ExecutionReports straight onto their own Chronicle queue, tailed by its own adapter and published into tick.q alongside the market data. Same mechanism, same envelope, same commit offset — a different message class and a different destination table. That table is execs, and it is where the next section begins. The symmetry is the point: once order flow and market data arrive on the same tickerplant as two schema'd streams, every benchmark in §4 becomes an aj between them. Keeping execution reports on a separate pipeline is the architectural decision that makes TCA hard later.

The convergence is not a coincidence so much as an admission: any system that must answer what happened, exactly, and in what order after the fact is forced into the same shape. Write the events down once, in order, and never edit them; treat every view as a fold you can recompute; make the consumer idempotent, because delivery will be at-least-once whether you planned for it or not. FIX reached that conclusion in the 1990s, Chronicle by making the log and the transport the same bytes, kdb+ by making the log the recovery path. What differs is only which half each optimises — FIX the wire, Chronicle the write, kdb+ the read.

Where does this leave kdb+? Precisely where the next section picks up. Chronicle owns the in-band path: the decision-critical microseconds between an order arriving and a child order leaving, in Java, with hard latency budgets. kdb+ owns the out-of-band plane: the joins, aggregations, time-series analytics, and history that make sense of what the in-band path did. The two compose naturally — a tailer on the Chronicle queues is just another feed handler publishing to the tickerplant — and mature desks run exactly that: Chronicle for the racing line, kdb+ for the telemetry. The architectures rhyme because they solved the same problem independently: an append-only log as the source of truth (queue file / TP log), single-writer appends, derived state rebuilt by replay, consumers decoupled from the hot path. Chronicle optimises the write side in microseconds; kdb+ optimises the read side in queries.

8 · The kdb+ view: lifecycle as event stream

The FIX discipline — immutable events, derived state — maps one-to-one onto a tickerplant architecture. The FIX gateway is just another feed handler: it parses ExecutionReports into a schema'd table and publishes to the tickerplant, which logs and fans out to subscribers. Order state lives in a CEP subscriber as a keyed table; the raw execs stream is the audit log, replayable from the TP log after any crash. Replay-from-log is the kdb+ answer to the FIX resend problem, and the two compose: idempotence on execid makes both session-level resends and TP log replays safe against double-application.

Ten Ways to Get This Wrong collects the failure modes that produce numbers which are arithmetically correct and practically false — VWAP alone, slippage without fill rate, one markout horizon, pooling internalised with lit flow. Five Rules is the general form of all of them: point-in-time always, one sign everywhere, cost never travels alone, no venue ranked on one metric, read curves not averages. If you take nothing else across from the companion, take those two pages.

Three design points carry the whole architecture. First, the four levels of Figure 3 do not need four tables. A parent is simply a row of ord whose poid equals its own oid; an algoChild is a row whose poid points elsewhere; a placement lives in place carrying the oid of the algoChild that spawned it; and a fill in execs carries both. One self-referencing table plus two event streams expresses the whole hierarchy, and — the point of the exercise — lets you measure parent-to-algoChild slippage and algoChild-to-placement slippage as two different group-bys over the same rows. If your platform tags algo decisions explicitly, an algoId or sliceId column on both ord and execs is the less clever and more legible alternative. Either works; what does not work is flattening to parent-and-fill, because then the question "algo or router?" has no column to answer it.

Second, events are the source of truth: the ord table can be rebuilt at any time by replaying execs from the TP log, so a crashed CEP recovers with zero data loss and no reconciliation against the OMS. Third, parent and child are the same schema: poid makes rollup a group-by rather than a join, and the invariant checks — qty=cumQty+leaves per order, parent cumQty equal to the sum over children — become one-line queries that run continuously as alerts. The same execs stream also feeds the trade lifecycle: filter execType in "FGH" and you have the fills/corrections/busts feed for allocation, confirm matching, and end-of-day recs — no second ingestion path, no drift between front- and middle-office numbers.

Key concept — the chained-tickerplant firewall

A tickerplant is single-threaded and publishes to subscribers in turn, so the slowest consumer sets the pace for everyone. Analytics are inherently bursty — a long query blocks the socket for its duration — which makes TCA a hazard to the live blotter if it subscribes directly. A chained tickerplant (log-less, fan-out only) interposes a buffer: the main plant sees one fast, well-behaved subscriber, and anything slow behind the chain can only ever back up the chain. The rule generalises beyond kdb+ — never let an analytical consumer share a backpressure path with an operational one.

Key concept — one stream, many folds

OMS order state, EMS child tracking, TCA, allocation input, and reconciliation are all different folds over the same immutable ExecutionReport stream. Persist the stream once at the tickerplant; let every consumer derive its own view. Divergence between systems then becomes impossible by construction rather than detected by reconciliation.

Summary: the OMS owns intent (parent orders, compliance, allocation), the EMS owns interaction with the market (child orders, algos, routing), FIX carries both lifecycles over one wire with ExecutionReport straddling the seam, and post-trade converts promises into finality through netting and DvP. Underneath, the same design keeps being rediscovered: an append-only event stream as the source of truth with derived, replayable state — enforced by FIX's ExecType discipline on the wire, by Chronicle's memory-mapped queues on the in-band path, and by the tickerplant log in kdb+ on the analytical plane. Systems built this way survive their own crashes; systems that aren't get reconciled by hand at midnight.
